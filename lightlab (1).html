01 · Reflection

Reflection of Light

When light bounces off a surface instead of passing through it. How evenly it bounces depends entirely on the surface.

Definition & types of reflection +

Reflection is the phenomenon of light bouncing back into the same medium after striking a surface.

Regular (specular) reflection

Parallel rays stay parallel after striking a smooth, polished surface (e.g. a plane mirror) — you get a sharp, clear image.

Diffuse reflection

Parallel rays scatter in many directions off a rough surface (paper, cloth, a wall) — no clear image forms, but this is why we can see non-shiny objects at all.

Laws of reflection +
  • The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal at the point of incidence all lie in the same plane.
  • The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection: \(\angle i = \angle r\), always measured from the normal — never from the mirror surface.
Common mistakeStudents often measure angles from the mirror surface instead of the normal. Examiners specifically check this in diagrams — always draw the normal as a dashed line first.

02 · Spherical Mirrors

Concave, Convex & Plane Mirrors

A spherical mirror is a piece cut from a hollow sphere, silvered on one side. Which side reflects decides everything.

🪟

Plane mirror

Flat reflecting surface. Image is always virtual, erect, laterally inverted, and the same size — formed as far behind the mirror as the object is in front.

🥄

Concave mirror

Reflects from the inner curved surface (like the inside of a spoon) — converges parallel rays to a real focus. Used in torches, headlights, solar cookers, shaving mirrors.

🔮

Convex mirror

Reflects from the outer curved surface — diverges rays, always gives a virtual, erect, diminished image with a wide field of view. Used as vehicle rear-view mirrors.

Key terms: pole, focus, centre of curvature +
  • Pole (P) — the centre point of the mirror's reflecting surface.
  • Centre of curvature (C) — the centre of the sphere the mirror was cut from.
  • Radius of curvature (R) — distance from pole to centre of curvature, \(PC\).
  • Principal axis — the straight line through P and C.
  • Principal focus (F) — the point on the principal axis where rays parallel to it converge (concave) or appear to diverge from (convex) after reflection.
  • Focal length (f) — distance \(PF\). For spherical mirrors, \(f = R/2\).

Mirror formula

Formula, magnification & a worked numerical

Mirror formula
1/v + 1/u = 1/f
Linear magnification
m = h′/h = −v/u
Worked numerical +

Q. An object 4 cm tall is placed 25 cm from a concave mirror of focal length 15 cm. Find the image position, size and nature.

Solution. u = −25 cm, f = −15 cm (concave).
1/v = 1/f − 1/u = −1/15 − (−1/25) = −1/15 + 1/25 = (−5+3)/75 = −2/75
v = −37.5 cm
m = −v/u = −(−37.5)/(−25) = −1.5 → h′ = m×h = −1.5×4 = −6 cm

Answer: Image is 37.5 cm in front of the mirror, real, inverted, 6 cm tall (magnified).

Memory trickFor a concave mirror, remember "SAME SIDE, SAME SIGN, REAL & INVERTED" for object between C and infinity — as the object moves closer to F, the image moves further from the mirror and grows.
03 · Refraction

Refraction of Light

Light bends when it crosses into a medium where it travels at a different speed — that's it, that's the whole idea.

Laws of refraction & Snell's Law +
  • The incident ray, refracted ray and normal all lie in the same plane.
  • Snell's Law: the ratio \( \sin i / \sin r \) is a constant for a given pair of media — called the refractive index.
Snell's law
n₁ sin i = n₂ sin r

🔬 Live: Snell's Law + Total Internal Reflection

⚠ Beyond the critical angle: no refracted ray exists — this is total internal reflection, the principle behind optical fibres.

Medium pairRefractive index (n)
Vacuum → Air1.0003 ≈ 1
Air → Water1.33
Air → Crown glass1.52
Air → Dense flint glass1.65
Air → Diamond2.42
Exam tipRefractive index has no unit — it's a ratio of two speeds (or two sines). A very common one-mark question.
Common mistakeBending "towards the normal" happens when entering a denser medium (e.g. air → glass); "away from the normal" happens when entering a rarer medium (e.g. glass → air). Mixing these up is the #1 refraction error in exams.
04 · Spherical Lenses

Convex & Concave Lenses

A lens is bound by two curved (or one curved, one flat) surfaces. Two refractions happen — one at each surface — but we treat the lens as a single thin unit.

🔍

Convex (converging) lens

Thicker at the centre than the edges. Converges parallel rays to a real focus on the far side. Used in magnifying glasses, cameras, projectors, and to correct hypermetropia (far-sightedness).

👓

Concave (diverging) lens

Thinner at the centre than the edges. Diverges parallel rays so they appear to come from a focus on the same side. Used to correct myopia (near-sightedness).
